PAYMENTS · CASE 01Reliability × Trust

Reducing payment-failure friction in Paytm

How might we make a failed payment feel recoverable instead of stressful?

Users may encounter bank downtime, network issues, limits, authentication errors or uncertain transaction status. A generic failure message leaves users unsure whether money moved and what to do next.

Frequent UPI users paying merchants, bills or peers—often under time pressure.

Specific failure states, clear money status and a single next-best action will improve safe recovery and user confidence.

Recovery rate · repeat-attempt success · time-to-resolution · support contacts per failed payment.

Read detailed case study +

01 — User journey

Start → enter amount → authenticate → processing → success/failure. The highest-risk moment is after failure: users need to know what happened to their money, whether to retry, and what to do next.

02 — Root-cause framework

  • Temporary technical failure: bank/network unavailable.
  • Actionable failure: limit, authentication or incorrect details.
  • Unknown status: user cannot tell whether the transaction is pending, reversed or failed.

03 — Proposed product solution

  • Contextual error cards: explain the likely cause in plain language.
  • Money-status banner: clearly show “not debited”, “pending” or “refund initiated” when applicable.
  • Next-best action: retry, change route, wait, or get help.
  • Retry guardrail: prevent duplicate payment attempts when status is uncertain.

04 — Experiment

A/B test the existing failure experience against the contextual recovery flow. Primary metric: successful recovery within 10 minutes. Guardrails: duplicate-payment complaints, support contacts and refund complaints.

05 — PM takeaway

Payment reliability is not only an infrastructure problem. Product design can reduce uncertainty and help users recover safely when infrastructure fails.

E-COMMERCE · CASE 02Conversion × Intent

Reducing e-commerce cart abandonment on Myntra

How might we help high-intent shoppers cross the last mile from “I want this” to “place order”?

Users can abandon after investing effort in discovery, sizing and cart creation. Friction may come from price surprises, delivery uncertainty, login, payment or decision fatigue.

High-intent shoppers with one or more items in their bag, especially mobile users making quick purchase decisions.

Reducing uncertainty and unnecessary checkout steps should increase completed orders without depending on blanket discounts.

Cart-to-order conversion · checkout completion · checkout time · payment failure · cancellation/return rate.

Read detailed case study +

01 — Funnel

Product view → add to bag → bag review → address/login → delivery → payment → order confirmation. Measure drop-off at each step rather than treating abandonment as one problem.

02 — Opportunity

The checkout should answer the user's biggest questions early: final price, delivery date, return policy, size confidence and payment options.

03 — Proposed product solution

  • Transparent total: show the final payable amount before the last step.
  • Delivery confidence: show estimated arrival and return policy in the bag.
  • One-tap continuation: preserve address/payment context for returning users.
  • Progressive checkout: reduce unnecessary screens and communicate remaining steps.
  • Intent-aware reminders: recover high-intent carts without excessive notifications.

04 — Prioritization

Use RICE—reach, impact, confidence and effort. Start with transparency and checkout simplification because they affect high-intent users and are relatively easy to test.

05 — Experiment

Test simplified checkout against the baseline. Primary metric: completed orders per cart. Guardrails: cancellations, returns and customer-support contacts.

MOBILITY · CASE 03Speed × Match Rate

Reducing cab-booking confirmation time in low-supply areas

When supply is thin, how do we make booking feel fast and predictable?

In areas with fewer nearby drivers, the system can spend longer searching before confirming—or search and fail. Users interpret prolonged uncertainty as unreliability.

Riders requesting cabs from suburban or low-supply areas, particularly during off-peak periods.

A transparent search state, adaptive matching and useful alternatives can reduce actual and perceived confirmation time.

Median confirmation time · booking success · search-to-cancel rate · driver acceptance · completed-trip rate.

Read detailed case study +

01 — Define the metric

Measure from the “Book” tap to confirmed driver, segmented by geography, time, vehicle type and supply-demand ratio. Averages can hide the low-supply problem.

02 — Product levers

  • Predictive confirmation range: set expectations when supply is low.
  • Adaptive search radius: expand matching intelligently when nearby supply is insufficient.
  • Alternative options: surface another vehicle category or scheduled ride where appropriate.
  • Search-state feedback: show that matching is active instead of a frozen spinner.

03 — Trade-off

Expanding the radius may improve match rate but increase driver pickup distance. Optimize for successful completed trips—not confirmation speed alone.

04 — Experiment

Compare adaptive matching + transparent search state against baseline, segmented by low/medium/high supply. Primary metric: completed bookings; secondary: confirmation time.

FINTECH · CASE 04Activation × Retention

Getting first-time users to the “aha” moment in a personal-finance app

A product cannot retain users who never experience its core value.

New users can face too many setup choices before seeing value. Long onboarding creates drop-off before a meaningful action is completed.

First-time users who have installed the app but have not yet completed a core value action.

Organizing onboarding around one useful outcome will reduce time-to-value and improve early retention.

Activation rate · time-to-value · D1/D7 retention · onboarding completion.

Read detailed case study +

01 — Reframe onboarding

Instead of asking users to configure everything, identify the smallest action that demonstrates value. Defer advanced setup until after the first success.

02 — Proposed flow

  • Ask one intent question: “What do you want to improve?”
  • Recommend one starting action based on the answer.
  • Show immediate progress and a useful result.
  • Only then introduce advanced features and personalization.

03 — Experiment

Control: existing onboarding. Variant: goal-based onboarding. Primary metric: activation within the first session. Guardrails: permission opt-outs, onboarding abandonment and early uninstall rate.

04 — PM takeaway

Onboarding is not a tour of every feature. Its job is to get the user to value as quickly and safely as possible.
